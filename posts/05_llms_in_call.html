LLMs In The History of Call

2026 Hilary Term

A Paradigm Shift in Interaction

In the last two years, Computer-Assisted Language Learning has undergone its most radical transformation yet. We have moved from "pre-scripted" CALL—where learners interacted with rigid, rule-based systems—to "Generative" CALL. The catalyst? Large Language Models (LLMs). These models don't just recognize language; they understand intent, simulate personae, and generate infinite variations of comprehensible input.

The LLM as a "Super Tutor"

The primary shift has been the emergence of LLMs as sophisticated pedagogical agents. Unlike previous iterations of chatbots, modern models act as scalable and personalized tutors capable of simulating complex conversational scenarios. Research from early 2025 suggests that LLMs are now effectively functioning as "data enhancers," generating culturally rich and learner-specific reading materials on the fly, which was previously a massive bottleneck for human instructors.

Feedback: Timing and Personalization

One of the most exciting developments is the nuance in Corrective Feedback (CF). Traditionally, digital feedback was binary. Today, LLMs allow for "negotiation of meaning." A 2026 study on immediate vs. delayed feedback in LLM-powered chatbots revealed that these systems can now adapt their feedback style based on the learner’s specific proficiency level and personality. This mimics the scaffolding behavior of a human teacher, providing "recasts" or "negative evidence" depending on what will best support the learner’s current i+1 trajectory.

The "Agentic" Turn

We are also seeing the rise of LLM Agents—systems that don't just talk, but plan. As detailed in recent findings on LLM agents in education, these tools use long-term memory to track a student’s recurring errors over weeks of interaction. This allows the AI to "plan" future conversations to target those specific weaknesses, turning a simple chat into a structured, longitudinal curriculum.

The Ethical Horizon

However, this "Golden Age" comes with caveats. As we integrate these models, the CALL community is grappling with what researchers call technological readiness and ethicality. Concerns regarding "hallucinations" (where the AI provides incorrect grammar rules confidently) and the lack of transparency in how these models are trained remain significant hurdles for formal institutional adoption.

Conclusion

The past two years have proved that LLMs are not just a trend; they are the new infrastructure of CALL. By providing a safe, high-variability environment for output, they are finally delivering on the promise of truly individualized language acquisition at scale.
