What is Le(T)TAT?

March 16th 2026 Hilary Term

When we build digital tools for language learning, it’s easy to get distracted by the "shiny" factor, i.e., what is the newest AI, the smoothest graphics, or the most complex game mechanics. But, how do we then ensure these tools used are actually helping people learn a language?

This is where the Le(T)TAT Framework (formerly known as TATL) comes in to play (developed by our very own Neasa Ní Chiaráin). It provides a blueprint for answering this exact question. It moves us away from focusing solely on the "computer", rather toward a unified, interdisciplinary approach to the development of CALL applications.

What is Le(T)TAT?

The LeTAT framework is an initial analysis tool designed to help developers evaluate or plan CALL (Computer-Assisted Language Learning) platforms. The acronym stands for:

  • Learner (and Teacher)
  • Theory
  • Action
  • Technology

1. The Learner... for whom?

We often treat all language app users as a simple, homogeneous entity, but Le(T)TAT reminds us that learning happens in a complex environment. To design a tool that works, we must consider multiple things about the learner, such as:

  • Existing Proficiency: Is the level appropriate?
  • Cognitive Style: How does the individual process information?
  • Sociocultural Background: Does the content resonate with their reality?
  • Willingness to Engage: Are they motivated to use this specific technology to acquire the target language?

2. Theory... why?

Theory is what helps us know if what we’re doing treads water, i.e., if what we are creating can actually be impactful, or simply another toy. In CALL, common theories include Input Hypothesis developed by Stephen Krashen, Interactionist Hypothesis developed by Michael Long, and the Output Hypothesis developed by Merrill Swain, among others.

The choice of activity should always be rooted in a theory of language acquisition. For decades, the dominant approach has been the Behaviourist model by B.F. Skinner, focusing on the drill and kill method, which focuses on intense, repetitive practice.

3. Action... what?

This refers to the tasks or activities the system promotes. Are we asking for simple repetitive exercises, or are we building complex interactive games and virtual worlds? Ideally, these actions should involve task-based learning with real-life or simulated engagement between the learner and the system.

4. Technology... how?

Technology acts as the delivery vehicle. This includes tools like:

  • TTS (Text-to-Speech)
  • ASR (Automatic Speech Recognition)
  • NLP (Natural Language Processing)

Why it matters

The Le(T)TAT framework is essential because it ensures that language learning tools are fit for purpose by bridging the gap between technical design, pedagogical theory, and the actual needs of the learner. It provides a structured, interdisciplinary plan that moves away from using the "shiny", “new” technology, towards prioritising learning outcomes.
