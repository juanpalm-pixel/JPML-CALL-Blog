Speech Synthesis

February 16th 2026 Hilary Term

Over the past five years, advances in neural text‑to‑speech (TTS) have moved from “good enough” synthetic voices to highly natural, controllable speech that CALL designers can use in new ways. Modern TTS is no longer just a convenience for generating audio: it is a pedagogical tool that can be adapted for pronunciation models, multimodal reading tasks, and scalable listening practice.

Two technical trends have driven this shift. First, neural TTS architectures (waveform neural vocoders, end‑to‑end acoustic models) have dramatically improved naturalness and prosodic control; a recent survey of neural speech synthesis summarizes these breakthroughs and their implications for downstream applications. A Survey on Neural Speech Synthesis (Tan et al., 2021).

Second, research into controllable and multi‑speaker TTS (including accent and emotion control) has made it feasible to generate targeted models for pedagogical aims: exaggerated prosody for perception training, slowed but natural speech for early learners, or multiple accents for exposure tasks. Work on modular, self‑supervised TTS models shows how smaller datasets and disentangled representations can produce flexible voices suitable for CALL prototypes. ParrotTTS: Disentangled Representations for TTS (2024).

Pedagogically, three affordances stand out. First, TTS enables rapid creation of parallel audio for graded reading: the same text can be rendered in different speaking rates, prosodic styles, or accents so learners can compare and notice differences. Second, TTS can be integrated with visual feedback (waveforms, pitch contours) to support pronunciation training without requiring a native speaker for every recording. Third, TTS scales: large reading corpora with aligned audio are now feasible for classroom platforms and adaptive apps.

Empirical work from the last few years suggests learners accept and benefit from AI‑powered TTS when it is well‑designed and paired with tasks that make listening active rather than passive. A mixed‑methods study of EFL learners’ perceptions of AI TTS apps found positive engagement and perceived gains in pronunciation when learners used TTS alongside guided practice sessions. EFL Students' Perception of AI TTS Apps (2024).

There are caveats. Many current CALL apps still use TTS as a drop‑in audio source rather than as a controllable pedagogical variable; feedback mechanisms remain uneven (binary scores vs. actionable guidance); and ethical questions about voice cloning and consent are emerging as TTS becomes more lifelike. Designers should pair TTS with explicit listening tasks, visual prosody displays, and opportunities for production and negotiation so that synthetic audio becomes a springboard for interaction rather than a passive resource.
